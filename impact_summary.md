# Economic contribution of an illustrative UK production slate

All inputs are illustrative. They show the outputs the model produces from production cost reports,
payroll and membership data. They do not describe any real production, studio, service or company.
Money figures are in the prices of the inputs, which are stated in 2023 prices.

## Headline

The four productions had combined budgets of £152.8m, of which £143.3m was spent
in the UK. Including their UK supply chains and the spending of the wages they paid, that spend
supported £156.3m of UK gross value added and 2,199 full-time equivalent jobs,
943 of them on the productions themselves as reported in payroll. Of the GVA, £109.0m was
paid to workers as wages, £47.1m was operating surplus earned by UK businesses and
the rest was taxes on production.

## Where the money was spent

56% of UK spend took place outside London, led by the South East and Scotland. These figures show where the
productions spent money, paid cast and crew and reported jobs. The ONS tables describe the UK as a
whole, so the model does not estimate supply-chain or household-spending effects for individual
nations and regions.

## Tax credit and the public finances

The drama series, feature film and animated series would qualify for the Audio-Visual Expenditure Credit; the
unscripted entertainment series does not, as unscripted entertainment is outside the scheme. The three
eligible productions would claim an estimated £39.5m of credit,
a net cost to the Exchequer of £29.6m once the credit is taxed. The activity they support
generates £44.3m of tax receipts, mostly income tax and National Insurance on the wages paid, or
£1.50 of gross receipts for every £1 of net relief.

These receipts are gross. They count tax on all the activity the productions support, including any
that would have taken place in the UK without the credit, so they measure contribution. The effect of
the credit on the public finances depends on two further shares: additionality, the share of the
activity that would not have taken place in the UK without the credit, and displacement, the share
of that additional activity that draws on workers and resources that would otherwise have been
employed elsewhere in the UK. With no displacement, the receipts caused by the credit cover its cost
once additionality reaches 67%. With 25% displacement the threshold rises to
89%. At 50% additionality and no displacement, the credit would cost the
Exchequer £7.5m net. Across the model settings tested, the gross return ranges from
£1.16 to £1.63 per £1, and the widest range comes from
induced effects.

## Value to members and the incidence of a levy

For a hypothetical service with 10 million members paying £10.00 a month and a
membership price elasticity of 1.2, members receive about £500m a year of value above
what they pay (linear demand, the conservative case). A 5% levy on subscription revenue
would raise about £60m a year. A service that sets its price to maximise profit, with a
cost of serving each member consistent with that elasticity, would pass on about 9% of
the levy. Its price would rise by about 4p a month, around
53 thousand members would leave, members would lose £5m of value
and the service would bear £60m. A levy on revenue takes the same share of revenue
at every price, so it gives a service with low running costs little reason to raise its price. If
the service passed the levy on in full, about 600 thousand members would leave and
the combined loss to members and the service would exceed the levy raised by
£62m.

## Basis

Static input-output model of the UK economy built from the ONS Input-Output Analytical Tables 2023,
reproducing the ONS published Type I effects. Induced effects use a household closure based on the
OECD tax wedge and ONS saving ratio for 2023. Tax receipts use average effective rates and are gross.
Credit calculations follow the AVEC rules in a single-period simplification and assume every eligible
production is certified as British.
